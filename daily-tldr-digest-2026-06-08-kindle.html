The Recipe Layer

When the deliverable is no longer code or seats but the recipe that produces an outcome, every other layer of the SaaS contract has to renegotiate itself.

§1 · ORIENTATIONThe new deliverable

Twelve sources, one quiet thesis: the unit of work the software industry sells is changing under us, and the rest of the stack — pricing, org chart, designer's craft, legal posture, infrastructure consolidation — is the second-order consequence still being negotiated.

For thirty years the deliverable was code that runs. For the SaaS era it was seats that log in. The pieces in today's reading describe a third regime, where the deliverable is the recipe: a versioned bundle of system prompts, datasets, evals, retrieval policies, and tool grants that produces an outcome when you point it at a problem. The code is incidental. The seats are vestigial. The recipe is the asset.

This sounds like a vocabulary game, and partly it is. But the surface area of disagreement across today's twelve sources — from a UX essay on delegation to a SaaS CFO on outcome pricing to a copyright lawyer on training-data liability — is mostly about where the recipe lives, who owns it, and how you charge for what it does. Those are not the questions a "code shop" or a "SaaS vendor" was organised to answer.

If you treat the recipe as the primary artefact, four things follow naturally and recur across the reading: (1) the designer's job is to define what gets delegated and what stays human; (2) the PM's job is GitHub-shaped, because recipes are text and text wants version control; (3) the pricing model has to attach to the outcome the recipe produces, because customers can't audit the recipe itself; (4) the legal exposure attaches to the training corpus and retrieval surface, because that's where the recipe gets its judgment from.

Three signals from today's reading

  1. The "AI-native designer" is a delegation discipline, not a tool stack. The job moved from drawing screens to deciding which judgments a model is allowed to make on the user's behalf — and which it must escalate.
  2. Outcome-based pricing is the SaaS reset, not a niche experiment. Per-seat economics break the moment the AI does the work of three seats; the SaaS CFO piece argues vendors who don't move will be picked off by ones that do.
  3. "Recipes" want GitHub-style workflows. System prompts, datasets, and evals are text files in everything but name. PR review, branch protection, and CI gates are migrating from code into the recipe layer — and the PM owns the merge button.

§2 · INFRASTRUCTURERecipes, not code

Two pieces sit at the centre of the day's thesis. The GitHub for AI Product Managers essay argues that the new PM craft is recipe stewardship: the system prompt is a spec, the eval suite is a test plan, the retrieval index is a dependency, and all of them want PRs, reviewers, semantic versioning, and a release process. Calling them "AI configs" undersells how much of the product's behaviour now lives in those files. The author's sharpest move is to point out that the most consequential decision a PM ships in 2026 is usually a prompt edit, not a feature flag.

The Code-as-Content Era piece comes at the same shift from the producer side: with one-shot generation collapsing the marginal cost of a software artefact toward zero, software starts to behave like content. It has the lifecycle of a meme — a hot week, a meme-aware audience, a churn rate that makes "long-term maintenance" look like a category error. Most of what gets generated is disposable on purpose. The implication is uncomfortable but earned: if your competitive edge is "we ship features," the recipe layer is going to eat you, because anyone with the recipe can ship the feature too.

Both pieces are descriptions of the same pivot from a different vantage. The PM piece tells you how the artefact wants to be managed; the Code-as-Content piece tells you how the artefact's economics have moved. Together they explain why durable advantage migrates upstream — to the recipe, the data, the evals, the legal posture — and downstream, to distribution.

§3 · DATARetrieval is a product decision

The Retrieval-Layer essay is the day's most consequential infrastructure piece. Its claim is sharper than the standard RAG explainer: the choice of what the model gets to read at inference time is now the single biggest determinant of the product's output, and that choice is upstream of the model, the prompt, and the UI. It is therefore the PM's problem, not the platform team's.

Three implications worth surfacing:

  • Retrieval is the truth budget. Whatever the retrieval policy admits is what the system can be right about. Whatever it excludes is what it will hallucinate around. The policy is a product spec disguised as plumbing.
  • Retrieval is the bias surface. If your corpus over-represents one source class — vendor docs, blog posts, support tickets — the system inherits that worldview. There is no neutral retrieval; there are only retrieval choices you can defend.
  • Retrieval is the legal surface. Every doc you pull in is a doc you've decided is fair to use. The copyright piece (§7) sits exactly on top of this point.

The recipe-layer framing makes the retrieval policy a versioned, reviewable artefact: which sources, with what freshness, weighted how, filtered by what. Once you treat it that way, "the chatbot got it wrong" stops being a model complaint and starts being a retrieval bug — which is much more fixable, and much more obviously someone's job. ↳ Continues Jun 5 — The Legible Stack's point that the 2026 stack is legible because the recipe (prompts, retrieval, evals) is plain text you can read.

§4 · PRICINGOutcome-based pricing and the services reset

Ben Murray's SaaS CFO piece is the operations-side translation of everything above. If a single AI agent inside the buyer's company displaces three users of your tool, per-seat pricing means your revenue shrinks while your product is winning. That is not a wrinkle. That is the central pricing tension of the next five years, and the post lays out a usable taxonomy of how to move:

  1. Per-outcome. Charge per resolved ticket, per qualified lead, per closed-loop workflow. Best when the outcome is countable and audit-friendly.
  2. Per-action. Charge per call to the recipe — close to usage pricing but anchored to the unit of work, not the API call. Best when outcomes are too messy to count cleanly.
  3. Hybrid floor + outcome. A small platform fee that covers your fixed cost of being in the buyer's stack, plus outcome metering on top. The pattern most enterprise sales teams will actually be able to ship in 2026.

The corollary piece, How to Build an AI-Native Services Company, takes the same logic to its endpoint: if you can sell the outcome reliably, you don't need to sell the software at all. You sell the work. The services-co thesis is no longer a downmarket consolation prize — it's a recognition that for many problems, the buyer doesn't want a tool, they want a result, and the AI-native services company is the cleanest way to deliver one. ↳ Continues the token-reckoning / services-as-software thread from Jun 4 — The Bifurcation and the AI-native stack frame in Jun 5 — The Legible Stack.

Both pieces converge on a corporate form where the recipe is the asset, the outcome is the SKU, and the team is small. The CFO has to learn to forecast revenue against a metric the customer agrees to in advance and the system instruments in real time. That is a real change to the financial model, not a label change on the invoice.

§5 · CRAFTThe AI-native designer and the judgment layer

The day's most interesting craft piece is Being an AI-native designer isn't what you think it is. The author's argument: the AI-native designer's job is not "design with AI tools" and definitely not "make the AI's UI prettier." It is delegation discipline. The designer decides, for each step in a user's task, whether the model should act on the user's behalf, propose and wait for confirmation, or refuse and escalate to a human reviewer. That choice is the design.

Two companion pieces sharpen the same point from adjacent angles. The Idearium reframes the design surface as a thinking space — a place for the user to wander, branch, and revisit, with the AI as quiet collaborator rather than auto-complete tyrant; the implication is that some surfaces should be slow and reflective on purpose. Why Visual Storytelling in UX Matters More Than Ever (UXPin) makes the more pragmatic case that as agents do more of the doing, the UI's job is increasingly to show its work: to make the model's plan, sources, and confidence visible so a human can intervene.

These three pieces, read together, give a useful four-band picture of what a 2026 product surface actually has to support.

Figure 2 · The 2026 product surface has to support all four bands. Most "AI features" only address Band 3 — which is exactly the band you should worry about least.

The designer who only thinks about Band 3 — "make the agent feel magical" — ships a product that is delightful for thirty seconds and disastrous on day two. The Bands 1 and 4 work is most of the craft now.

§6 · INFRASTRUCTURE IIThe recipe layer's hidden dependencies

Two infra-shaped pieces today are worth reading as the recipe layer's plumbing: QVAC (tetherto's local-AI SDK for running LLMs, speech-to-text, and translation peer-to-peer on Linux / macOS / Windows / Android / iOS) and the VoidZero joining Cloudflare announcement.

QVAC is the more interesting of the two strategically. If recipes are the asset, the question of where the recipe gets executed matters enormously: a recipe that has to round-trip to a frontier model API for every invocation is a recipe whose economics, latency, and privacy are all hostage to a third party. A local-first SDK that runs the recipe on the user's device shifts that calculus. Don't read it as a model-host war; read it as the recipe layer trying to find an execution substrate that doesn't compromise the things that make recipes valuable.

The Cloudflare / VoidZero consolidation is the other end of the same problem: the toolchain layer (Vite, Vitest, Rolldown, oxc — the JS build chain VoidZero was unifying) is getting absorbed into a distribution-layer company. That's a vote that delivery, not compilation, is where the durable value is. For a recipe-layer business, that matters: the recipe runs on someone's edge, and Cloudflare wants the edge to be theirs.

A small companion observation: the Vercel Security Checkpoint source is, again, an artefact — NotebookLM ingested a CDN challenge page in place of the article it was pointed at. We saw the identical pattern on June 4. Treat it as a useful tell about how often the open web is now gated behind anti-bot interstitials that crawlers (and, increasingly, retrieval pipelines) silently fail on. The recipe-layer business that solves "what to do when your retrieval target is behind a checkpoint" will sell a lot of seats — or, more on-brand, a lot of outcomes. ↳ Same artefact appeared in Jun 4 — The Bifurcation; pattern, not coincidence.

§7 · LEGALCopyright, training data, and the legal substrate

The High Cost of the AI Copyright Illusion is the day's hardest read because it argues, persuasively, that the current vendor posture on training-data copyright — "we'll figure it out, fair use will hold, the courts haven't ruled, just keep shipping" — is a transferred liability rather than a resolved question. The author lays out three concrete costs being externalised onto buyers and onto the public:

  1. Indemnification asymmetry. Enterprise buyers are being asked to indemnify themselves against training-data claims they have no visibility into.
  2. Provenance opacity. Recipe-layer products that rely on retrieval over third-party corpora are inheriting copyright exposure from data they didn't ingest themselves, and often can't even enumerate.
  3. Settlement geometry. Where vendors do settle (publisher deals, content-licensing carve-outs), the settlements are structured to protect the vendor's balance sheet, not the downstream customer's.

Read against the retrieval-layer piece (§3), the implication is direct: every retrieval policy is also a legal posture, and the recipe-layer business has to make both choices explicitly. The "we use public web data" answer was acceptable when models were research artefacts. It is not acceptable when the recipe is the deliverable and the customer is paying for the outcome.

This is not abstract. The CFO move toward outcome pricing (§4) and the designer move toward delegation discipline (§5) both depend on a legal substrate that can survive an audit. The piece's quiet warning is that we are pricing and designing as if that substrate exists, while the law is still in the middle of deciding what it is.

§8 · PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYSIf you only do five things this week

  1. Inventory your recipes. Whatever prompts, retrieval policies, eval sets, and tool grants are currently powering your product — put them in a repo with PR review. If you can't list them in an hour, that's the size of the problem.
  2. Audit your retrieval policy. Write down what your system retrieves, from where, how stale it's allowed to be, and what's excluded. Treat the document as a product spec, not an ops note.
  3. Run an outcome-pricing thought experiment. Pick one workflow and ask what it would cost the customer if you charged per resolved outcome. The answer tells you whether your current pricing is leaving money on the table or about to collapse.
  4. Map your delegation stack. For your most important user task, identify which bands (Figure 2) currently happen where. Most products will find Band 3 over-built and Bands 1 and 4 under-supported.
  5. Trace one source through your training and retrieval pipelines. Pick a doc, follow it through ingest, embedding, retrieval, and prompt assembly. If you can't trace it end-to-end, your copyright posture is whatever your most permissive vendor's posture is, transitively.

§9 · GLOSSARY

Recipe layer
The versioned, reviewable bundle of system prompts, datasets, eval sets, retrieval policies, and tool grants that defines an AI product's behaviour. Distinct from model weights and from application code; managed with PM tools, not platform tools.
Outcome-based pricing
Charging for a verified business result (resolved ticket, qualified lead, completed workflow) rather than for software access. Replaces per-seat as the dominant SaaS pricing motion when one user can drive many resolved outcomes.
Delegation discipline
The designer's craft of deciding, for each step in a user task, whether the system should act autonomously, propose-and-wait, or escalate to a human. The defining skill of the AI-native designer.
Retrieval policy
The explicit spec for what an AI system gets to read at inference time — sources, freshness, ranking, exclusion. Upstream of the model, the prompt, and the UI; downstream of the product strategy.
Code-as-content
The thesis that one-shot AI code generation has collapsed software's marginal cost to near zero, making most software artefacts disposable and meme-shaped rather than durable and capital-intensive.
AI-native services company
An organisational form that sells outcomes (not seats, not software) and uses AI internally to deliver them at SaaS-like margins. The terminal endpoint of the outcome-pricing logic.
