Volume II · Hype Meets the Ledger

Mark to Market

Eight sources, one verdict: the AI industry is being marked to market. Ed Zitron tallies an infrastructure bill the revenue can't cover; Simon Willison clocks Claude Fable 5 still bending the capability curve upward; a stack blueprint, the context-engineering turn, and the rise of "agent experience" professionalize how the thing gets built; and answer-engine optimization, seven-day subscriptions, and Apple's quiet default show what selling AI looks like once the hype is priced out.

8 sources · 3 diagrams · ~22 min read Distilled from NotebookLM · personal reading log

§1 · OrientationTwo moods that turn out to be one story

Today's eight sources split cleanly into two moods that, read together, are the same argument. Half are about money getting serious: an infrastructure bill that has outrun revenue, subscription plans engineered to extract cash from skeptics, an optimization discipline for being the answer an LLM hands back. The other half are about craft getting serious: a named blueprint for the modern stack, prompt engineering maturing into context engineering, developer experience reframed as agent experience, and a frontier model that is still, demonstrably, getting better. The connective tissue is accountability. 2026 is the year AI stops being valued on the story it tells and starts being valued on what it can be shown to deliver — in dollars, and in benchmarks.

The AI-bubble thread has run through this archive since Jun 4's The Bifurcation; today the question gets sharper — how much of "AI is slowing down" survives once you separate the financing curve from the capability curve, and which of the two is Fable 5 actually on?

Six agreements across today's sources

  1. The metric changed. Promise stopped counting. Revenue, conversion rates, and benchmark deltas are now the unit of judgment across every source — the financial ones and the technical ones alike.
  2. The bill outran the revenue. Zitron's tally: roughly $725B in 2026 infrastructure capex against frontier-lab revenues an order of magnitude smaller. The fragility lives in the financing, not the technology.
  3. "Slowing down" is an economic claim, not a technical one. Willison's Fable 5 notes — a two-month Stripe migration done in a day, a 90% on Hex's hardest analytics benchmark — are evidence the capability curve is still bending up while the money curve wobbles. The two camps are graphing different lines.
  4. The build layer is professionalizing under one move. The modern-stack blueprint, context engineering replacing prompt engineering, and agent experience replacing developer experience are the same migration: the unit of engineering is no longer the model call but the environment the agent works inside.
  5. Go-to-market got hard-nosed. Answer-engine optimization to capture LLM-mediated demand and seven-day subscriptions to monetize the wary are revenue tactics for a market that no longer buys on faith.
  6. Distribution is the consumer endgame. Apple's bet isn't being best; it's being the default. The channel, not the model, decides who reaches the mainstream.

§2 · StructureThe stack finally gets a blueprint

The day's most structural source lays the modern AI system out as a layered diagram: compute and infrastructure at the base, foundation models above them, then orchestration and routing, then the context-and-data layer, then agents and tools, and the product surface on top. The value of the exercise is not the inventory — every consultancy has a stack diagram now — but what the layering reveals once you ask which layers are yours. The model layer, the one that dominated the 2024 conversation, is the most rented part of the whole structure: you do not own it, you do not differentiate on it, and your competitor can call the same endpoint by lunch.

The synthesis the blueprint implies, and that no single layer states, is a direction of travel: value is migrating up and to the sides, away from the model and into the layers that are proprietary to you — your context and data, your orchestration policy, your product surface. That is the moat-migration thread this archive has tracked for two weeks, now drawn as an org chart. Read defensively, the blueprint is a map of where not to plant your flag.

Continues Jun 5 — The Legible Stack's argument that the stack is becoming legible enough to reason about layer by layer. Today's blueprint adds the vector: legibility was step one; knowing which layers hold value as the model commoditizes is step two.

Figure 1 · The modern AI stack, read for ownership rather than inventory. The two black bands at the top — context and product surface — are the only layers a competitor can't rent by lunchtime, which is precisely why value is migrating into them and out of the model.

§3 · CraftFrom prompts to environments: context engineering and AX

Two sources describe the same shift from opposite ends. The Context Engineering Revolution argues that the job has moved from how to ask — the prompt-engineering question — to what the agent knows, sees, and remembers at the moment it acts. Context stops being prose you tune and becomes infrastructure you manage: dynamic selection, compression, memory, retrieval scope, policy. The piece traces the term to Andrej Karpathy's mid-2025 coinage and makes the operating-system analogy explicit — the developer's job is deciding what gets paged into the model's limited working memory at each step.

Builder.io's Agent experience is the new developer experience is the same migration seen from the design side. Where DX optimized the human's path through a codebase, AX optimizes the agent's: the context, tools, permissions, tests, and review loops that tell an autonomous worker what matters, what it may touch, and how it knows it succeeded. Its sharpest observation is that with agents, onboarding happens at the start of every single task — there is no ramp-up that persists — so the repo's instructions, schemas, and known failure modes have to teach the worker from scratch, every time, through a deterministic and bounded preview environment.

The synthesis worth keeping, present in neither piece alone: context engineering and agent experience are one discipline wearing two job titles. Both relocate the engineering effort from the model call to the environment the agent inhabits; both are in the business of building a deterministic world for a stochastic worker. One approaches it as data plumbing, the other as interaction design, and they meet in the middle.

Continues Jun 10 — The Harness Era's harness thread. Context engineering is the harness seen from the data side; agent experience is the harness seen from the design side. Same machine around the model, named twice this week.

§4 · EconomicsThe bill comes due

Ed Zitron's AI Is Slowing Down is the day's loudest source and its bleakest. The core arithmetic: the four largest hyperscalers are on track to spend on the order of $725 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026, while the two flagship model companies post annualized revenues — roughly $25B for OpenAI, $19B for Anthropic by his accounting — that sit an order of magnitude beneath the capital being poured into the ground to serve them. He marshals the bear-case furniture: revenue growth decelerating, circular vendor-customer investments, power and data-center capacity built for a demand curve that may not arrive, and the live risk that 2024–2025-vintage assets strand by 2027–2028. Impairments, he notes, have already started being taken.

The synthesis that matters — and that separates a useful reading from doomscrolling — is to name which bubble this is. If there is one, it is a financing bubble, not a usefulness bubble. The danger Zitron actually documents is in the capital structure: debt-funded buildout, demand booked ahead of revenue, returns assumed rather than observed. That structure can break even if the technology keeps working perfectly — which is exactly what makes §5 the necessary other half of the page.

CounterZitron's case overshoots at one specific joint: "the demand won't come" is the weakest plank, and it is undercut twice on today's own page — by Fable 5's capability gains (§5) and by the go-to-market machinery being built to manufacture and capture demand (§6). The honest residue of his argument is narrower and harder to dismiss: the financing is fragile. That claim survives every counter on this page.

Continues Jun 4 — The Bifurcation's token-reckoning thread (82¢ of every AI dollar wasted; cost-per-completed-workflow as the honest metric). Zitron zooms that same waste out from the invoice to the balance sheet.

Figure 2 · Drawn to scale, the gap is the thesis. The question isn't whether AI is used — it's whether the revenue on the right can ever service the debt that built the box on the left. A financing problem, not a usefulness one.

§5 · Counter-curveMeanwhile, the model is still getting better

Where Zitron measures the money, Simon Willison measures the model — and on his bench it is still climbing. His Initial impressions of Claude Fable 5 — Anthropic's most powerful generally-available model, the public-access sibling of the more tightly held Mythos 5 — calls it a "beast": slow and expensive, but handling essentially everything he throws at it. The reported field results are the part worth holding next to §4. Stripe is said to have compressed a codebase-wide migration that would have taken a team over two months into a single day; the analytics company Hex reported Fable as the first model to clear 90% on its core benchmark for hard analytical work, with unusual judgment on the nuanced questions.

This is the reconciliation the day requires. "Slowing down" is a statement about revenue-per-dollar-of-capex, not capability-per-model-generation. The two curves are genuinely independent, and most bubble arguments quietly swap one for the other. Willison's notes are direct evidence the capability curve has not plateaued — which neither proves nor refutes Zitron, because they are about different graphs.

CounterBut the curves touch at one joint, and it cuts against the optimists. If frontier capability arrives "slow and expensive," then better models that cost more don't close Zitron's revenue gap — they can widen it. A capability curve that bends up only at frontier cost is not a refutation of the financing problem; it's a contributor to it. The comfortable reading ("the tech works, so the money will follow") assumes a cost trajectory Fable 5's own reviewer doesn't grant.

§6 · Go-to-marketHow you actually sell AI once the hype is priced out

Three sources, read together, are a single playbook for making money in a market that has stopped paying for promises. LLMs are picking winners. Here's how to become one. (PostHog) treats the model itself as the new demand channel: as buyers ask ChatGPT and Perplexity instead of Google, the game becomes answer-engine optimization — be the answer, not a blue link. Its concrete advice is unsentimental: get mentioned in the sources LLMs already cite, and measure your brand's appearance by asking the engines fifteen to twenty buying-intent questions a month and recording how you show up.

RevenueCat's Why 7-day plans are winning attacks the same problem at the till. Weekly subscriptions now drive a majority of in-app subscription revenue — north of 55% by their data — and convert at 1.7× to 7.4× the rate of annual plans on the same paywall, because a seven-day commitment is the lowest possible barrier for a user who doesn't yet trust the product enough to marry it. The cost is churn, paid later; the win is conversion, banked now. It is, precisely, how you monetize doubt.

M.G. Siegler's Apple Wins Consumer AI By Default supplies the third lever: distribution. His argument is that Apple takes consumer AI not by building the best model but by being the default — riding a billion devices and a reservoir of consumer trust to make Siri AI the way the mainstream meets the technology, the way ChatGPT was the way the early adopters met it. The synthesis across all three, stated by none: in a marked-to-market industry the model is table stakes, and the contest moves to capture (AEO), conversion (weekly plans), and default (Apple). Channel and meter, not model, decide who survives the repricing.

CounterSiegler files his own objection, and it's the right one: the danger to Apple isn't that Siri AI fails to launch — it's that it launches and simply isn't very good. Default distribution is a colossal head start, not a substitute for the product working. The same caveat shrinks the other two plays: AEO and weekly pricing are amplifiers, and amplifying a product nobody re-subscribes to just buys you faster, more measurable churn.

§7 · The long viewTwo curves, told apart

Hold §4 and §5 in the same hand and the whole day resolves into a single instruction: separate the two curves. There is a capability curve — is the technology still improving? — and a capital curve — is the money that built it durable? In 2023–2024 the two were fused; capability was priced as destiny and the financing rode on it. In 2025–2026 they have come apart. Fable 5 says the capability curve is still bending up. Zitron says the capital curve is fraying. Both can be true at once, and most of the year's loudest arguments are just one curve being mistaken for the other.

Map today's sources onto the two axes and they cluster in one quadrant — and it is the uncomfortable one.

Figure 3 · The bubble debate is really an argument about which quadrant we're in. Today's sources don't disagree about the technology and the money — they disagree about which one the word "bubble" refers to. Both point, separately, at the starred quadrant: capability rising, financing fragile.

If that quadrant is where we are, the build advice falls out of it directly. Make things that get more valuable as tokens get cheaper, and that do not depend on the buildout staying solvent: the context you own, the distribution you control, and the revenue mechanics you run — which is, not coincidentally, exactly what §6's money plays are reaching for. The falsifiable version, for the ledger: if the financing-bubble read is right, the first visible cracks should be infrastructure write-downs and stranded-asset disclosures — data centers, power contracts — within two-to-three quarters, not a capability plateau. And expect at least one frontier lab to keep posting benchmark gains straight through the financial wobble. If instead the next two quarters bring a capability stall before any balance-sheet event, it is §5 that was wrong, not §4.

§8 · Practical takeawaysIf you only do six things this week

  1. Separate the two curves before you argue about the bubble. When someone says "AI is slowing down," ask whether they mean capability or capital. The first is falsified roughly weekly; the second is a real, open question. Don't let one stand in for the other — most of the noise is that substitution.
  2. Treat context as the deliverable, not the prompt. On your next agent build, spend the effort on what the agent knows, sees, may touch, and how it verifies — the deterministic world around the stochastic worker. That is what context engineering and AX both actually mean.
  3. Audit where your value sits on the stack. Map your product onto Figure 1. If your moat is at the model layer, it's rented; move weight up toward context and product surface, the bands a competitor can't call by lunchtime.
  4. Instrument for the answer engine now. Run PostHog's check: ask 15–20 buying-intent questions across ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google; record whether and how your brand appears; repeat monthly. LLM-mediated demand is already a measurable channel.
  5. Test a weekly plan — and model the churn, not just the lift. RevenueCat's numbers make a seven-day entry tier worth trying, but a plan that wins the paywall and loses the cohort is a treadmill. Decide which you're optimizing before you ship it.
  6. Stress-test your dependencies for solvency, not just uptime. If your product rides a demand-ahead-of-revenue buildout, write the one-page plan for a provider price hike or capacity pullback in 2027. The technology working does not mean its financing does.

§9 · GlossaryTerms used above, briefly

Mark to market
A finance term for revaluing a position at its current real worth rather than its assumed or purchase value. Today's organizing metaphor: AI is being repriced from the story it told to what it can be shown to deliver.
Capability curve vs capital curve
The two independent trends this digest insists on telling apart: whether the technology is still improving (capability) versus whether the money that built it is durable (capital). Most "bubble" arguments confuse the two.
Context engineering
The discipline of managing what an LLM knows, sees, and remembers at the moment it acts — context as infrastructure (selection, compression, memory, retrieval policy) rather than prose. The successor to prompt engineering; coined by Andrej Karpathy in mid-2025.
Agent experience (AX)
The agent-facing analogue of developer experience: the context, tools, permissions, tests, and review loops designed so an autonomous worker knows what matters and how it succeeded. Notable because an agent re-onboards at the start of every task.
Answer Engine Optimization (AEO)
Structuring content and brand presence so AI answer engines surface you as the direct answer, not a ranked link. SEO's successor for a world where the search result is a synthesized response.
Stranded asset
Infrastructure built for expected demand that never fully arrives, leaving it underused and written down. Zitron's worry for AI data centers and power contracts provisioned in 2024–2025 against a 2026 demand curve that may fall short.
Weekly subscription (and churn)
A seven-day billing tier that lowers the commitment barrier and lifts conversion sharply, at the cost of higher cancellation rates. The mechanism by which products monetize users who don't yet trust them.
